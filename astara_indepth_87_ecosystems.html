01The Thesis

The boundaries of the automotive industry are becoming increasingly difficult to define

The boundaries of the automotive industry are becoming increasingly difficult to define. Automakers that historically concentrated on designing, manufacturing and selling vehicles are now investing in autonomous mobility, artificial intelligence, semiconductors, robotics and advanced aerial mobility. The table records 37 strategic moves between 2016 and 2026, with 21 occurring from 2024 onwards. Although the database is a selected sample rather than a complete census of the industry, the recent acceleration suggests that these activities are moving from experimental projects towards more central elements of OEM strategy.

This should not be understood as random diversification into unrelated industries. Most of these sectors are connected by a common technological stack. Autonomous vehicles need artificial intelligence, high-performance processors, sensors and software platforms. Humanoid robots use many of the same technologies for perception, decision-making, motion control and interaction with the physical world. Advanced aerial mobility combines electric propulsion, lightweight manufacturing, automation and safety-critical software.

Artificial intelligence is therefore the connective tissue of the expansion: it appears in 16 of the 37 recorded cases when multi-sector projects are counted in every relevant category, compared with 13 cases involving autonomous mobility, ten involving semiconductors, and seven each involving robotics and advanced aerial mobility.

The competitive question is gradually changing from which company can manufacture the best car to which company can control the most valuable parts of the technology stack and apply them across vehicles, factories, robots and new mobility services.

The way OEMs are entering these sectors is also significant. Strategic partnerships and research alliances represent 15 of the 37 cases, making them the most frequent mechanism. This indicates that most manufacturers do not believe they can build every capability alone. Partnerships allow them to access specialist knowledge while sharing development costs and technological risk.

Joint ventures provide greater control when a capability becomes strategically important, while internal programmes suggest that some OEMs are beginning to treat artificial intelligence, chip design and robotics as core competencies rather than technologies that can simply be purchased from suppliers.

Repeated investments by the same companies reinforce this interpretation. Toyota has progressively expanded its relationship with Joby Aviation from venture investment to manufacturing cooperation and a joint venture. Volkswagen has developed parallel relationships with Horizon Robotics, XPeng and Qualcomm covering chips, automated driving and vehicle architecture. Mercedes-Benz has deepened its work with NVIDIA from an autonomous-driving computing platform to artificial intelligence, industrial digital twins and fleet-wide computing infrastructure.

These are not isolated financial bets; they are multi-year attempts to construct new capability platforms.
